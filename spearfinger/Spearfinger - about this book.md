# Spearfinger · ᎪᏍᏓᏱ ᏗᎦᎵᏘᏍᏙᏗ ᎦᏰᏌᏛᎢ

A summary of the Spearfinger picture book and the material in `Downloads/C02Z129YJWF2/SpearfingerTotal`, written 19 October 2026 from the notes, bios, glosses, PDFs and recordings in that folder, with Sara's answers on the points the folder does not state (Hartwell Francis's part, the narrator, the spelling Nannie, the printed title). ## The book in one paragraph

*Spearfinger* is a Cherokee-language picture book of the Spearfinger story, adapted and illustrated in cut paper by Luzene Hill, told in Cherokee by Nannie Taylor and edited and glossed by Hartwell Francis, published by Galohisdi Press, Cullowhee, North Carolina, in 2008 for Western Carolina University's Cherokee Language Program, with support from the Cherokee Preservation Foundation. It has 27 pages of story, each with one illustration and a few lines of Cherokee, and it was presented to Hunter Library at WCU on 23 April 2009 together with a narrated slideshow of the pages.

## The title and Spearfinger's Cherokee name

The title to use is the printed one: **ᎪᏍᏓᏱ ᏗᎦᎵᏘᏍᏙᏗ ᎦᏰᏌᏛᎢ** *gó:sda:yi digá:hltisdóhdi gá:ye:sadv́:'i*, glossed "sharp · sharp object (spear, something to stick with a point) · her finger", as on the December 2008 syllabary sheet and the March 2009 presentation gloss (Sara's decision, 19 October 2026). The -li- syllable stands for a slight stop in Nannie's pronunciation. The earlier files (the 4 March 2008 layout, the July 2008 draft) and the 22 February 2016 name notes write it without the li, **ᎪᏍᏓᏱ ᏗᎦᏘᏍᏙᏗ ᎦᏰᏌᏛᎢ**, and the 2016 note leans that way, but the book stays as printed. The same note records the other names: Mooney's *udlvta* "she has it sharp" (related to *udlvdalvda* "splinter"), which Tom has heard as her old proper name, and King's *yvgi uwasugahli* "needle her fingernail"; the book followed Nannie's lead. In the story she is soon just *sgina gosdayi gayesad(i)*, "that sharp-fingered one". (`SpearfingerName022216.docx`, `Spearfinger030408.pdf`, `SpfingerSyllabary121808.pdf`.)

## How the text was made (the dates in the file names)

1. **October 2007.** Luzene Hill's English adaptation in 27 numbered lines, one per page (`SpearfingerGSCher/spearfinger.doc`, 16 October 2007). The same text is on the 2016 English poster.
2. **November 2007.** Nannie Taylor's Cherokee version typed in phonetics, *gosdayi digatisdodi gayesadvi* (`SpearfingerGSCher/gosdayi digatisdodi gayesadvi.doc`, 8 November 2007), and her slow reading of it recorded on 26 November 2007 (`NTSS_sfinger02_112607slow15.mp3`, 26½ minutes). The gloss notes add that Sallie wrote the story out too.
3. **February to June 2008.** Interlinear glosses of every line, syllabary added (`spfinger_gloss020108.doc`, `spfinger_syllabary022708.doc`, `spfinger_gloss061208.doc`); a review with Nannie on 18 April 2008 in 17 sound files; the first printed layout on 4 March 2008 and a readable proof on 11 July 2008 (18 spreads, 8½ × 7 in, cover "Translation by Nonnie Taylor").
4. **July to December 2008.** Production under Hartwell Francis: paper and cover colour chosen, two pages broken out to make 40 pages, typos on pages 15 and 23 fixed, all Roman-alphabet text replaced with syllabary, colophon and end note written (notes of 15 July and 21 October 2008). On 17 December 2008 the long NTTB recordings were made (21, 8 and 6 minutes), and on 18 December the final gloss and syllabary sheets were produced with change marks (`SpfingerGloss121808.doc`, `SpfingerSyllabary121808.pdf`, 11 pages).
5. **March to April 2009.** Line clips cut from the recording (`SpearfingerLines031109`, 20 clips, 11 March 2009); a presentation gloss (4 March 2009); the 27 illustrations scanned at 1200 × 945 px on 21 April 2009 and 27 narration tracks recorded or cut one per page the same day (`SpearfingerSound/1–27 Audio Track.aiff`, 11.8 minutes in all), joined into **`Spearfinger.mov`**, a 640 × 480 slideshow of 11 min 51 s.
6. **23 April 2009.** Reception 5:30 to 6:30 at the Hunter Library Media Center: the Cherokee Language Program of Cherokee Studies presented *Spearfinger!* to Hunter Library and Special Collections and opened a display of Cherokee-language literature (`SpearfingerReception042309.pdf`).
7. **February 2016.** The English-text poster (`SpearfingerEnglishPoster.pdf`, 12 × 18 in) and the name notes.

## Where the story came from

The June 2008 gloss opens with a discussion of the story as told by Nannie Taylor. She first heard Spearfinger as an adult, from Kathi Littlejohn; she had not heard it, or the Tili Bone story, as a child. She and Sallie grew up with accounts of the little people, which were treated as sightings rather than stories, and Sallie would leave food out for them. Mostly Nannie heard Bible stories from her grandfather; Marvin Taylor told the rattlesnake's vengeance; Mary Ulmer (Goingback Chiltoskie's wife) read to Sallie from her book in the school library. There was little time for stories with the water, wood and beans to see to, and Sundays were church. Nannie began reading and hearing the stories when Cherokee came back into the schools in the late 1970s (1977 at the middle school). (`Spearfinger/spfinger_gloss061208.doc`.)

## The story on the 27 pages

Long ago the Cherokee lived in peace in the mountain forests, but one thing disturbed them: Spearfinger, an old monster with a finger as long as a dagger and shaped like an awl, skin like rock, hungry for children's livers, able to take the shape of an old grandmother. In spring she met children picking strawberries ("Come, little girl, let me comb your hair"), lulled them and took their livers, singing *tsuwela detsigisgo'i, su sa sai*. In autumn she watched for the smoke of the chestnut-leaf burning and hid in fog. The people held a council, dug a pit across the trail, lit a fire and hid in the laurels; an old woman came hobbling along, fell through, and turned back into Spearfinger. Arrows broke on her skin. A titmouse sang "un, un, un", the hunters heard *unahu*, heart, shot at her chest and failed, and cut out the bird's tongue, so the titmouse is a liar to this day. A chickadee lit on her right hand: her heart was in her palm. An arrow struck it and she died; the chickadee is the truth teller. They burned her on the fire, and out of the fire came songs of healing that the Cherokee remember still.

## The art

Cut-paper collage: flat coloured papers on white, with the cut shapes' own shadows visible in the scans (a mother and daughter walking into the corn with a basket; Spearfinger in a purple robe among dark green trees).
